Question 01

What water bodies in Pakistan are affected by climate change?

Pakistan sits at the crossroads of three major mountain ranges the Himalayas, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush giving it the largest concentration of glaciers outside the polar regions, These feed mighty rivers like the Indus, Jhelum, Cheniab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej. Further south, the Arabian Sea coastline stretches over 1,000 km, while inland lakes like Manchar Lake (the largest freshwater lake in South Asia) and Keenjhar Lake face mounting stress. Pakistan's wetlands — including the Indus Delta and coastal mangroves — are all under the grip of a warming climate.

  • Indus river basin covers 11.2% of Pakistan's land

Question 02

How is climate change affecting these water bodies?

Pakistan is warming at nearly twice the global average, triggering a cascade of water-related crises. Glaciers are melting at an accelerating rate, causing glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) — violent flood events that sweep downstream with little warning. River flows are becoming unpredictable: intense floods in wet seasons followed by severe drought in dry ones. Sea-surface temperatures in the Arabian Sea are rising, intensifying monsoon systems and super-cyclones. Groundwater tables are dropping as over-extraction meets reduced recharge from shrinking snowpacks.

Question 03

What changes are being seen in water levels, temperature, or quality?

Water levels across Pakistan's rivers fluctuate wildly. The Indus has seen record high flows during monsoon seasons due to glacial melt combined with extreme rainfall, only to run critically low in winter months. Lake Manchar has drastically shrunk due to reduced inflows and diversion of water. Water temperatures in the Arabian Sea have risen by 1–1.5°C, disrupting marine ecosystems. Quality has also deteriorated — flood-driven sediment, agricultural runoff, and untreated sewage mix into drinking water sources, raising disease risk. Saltwater from the sea is intruding inland through the Indus Delta, contaminating freshwater wells used by millions.

Question 04

How does climate change affect marine life and other animals?

Warmer, more acidic Arabian Sea waters bleach coral reefs and reduce fish populations critical to coastal communities. The Indus River Dolphin (Platanista gangetica minor) — a critically endangered species found only in Pakistan — is threatened by lower water levels and increased sediment. Mangrove forests in the Indus Delta, home to migratory birds and juvenile fish nurseries, are shrinking due to salinity intrusion and reduced freshwater flow. Increased flooding also displaces wildlife from habitat corridors, while droughts cause die-offs of fish in lakes and rivers.

Question 05

How do these changes affect people and communities?

The human cost of Pakistan's water crisis is enormous. Farmers who rely on the Indus irrigation system — one of the world's largest — face unpredictable harvests due to erratic river flows. Fishing communities along the Arabian Sea and inland lakes face collapsing catches. The 2022 super-floods — directly linked to climate change and glacial melt — submerged one-third of Pakistan's land area, destroying 2 million homes and killing over 1,700 people. Urban water scarcity is rising: Karachi, a city of 22 million, already faces chronic water shortages. Waterborne disease outbreaks follow every major flood.

Question 06

What are the main causes of these changes?

While Pakistan contributes less than 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions, it is ranked among the world's most climate-vulnerable nations. The causes of these water changes are both global and local. Globally, burning fossil fuels raises CO₂ concentrations, increasing atmospheric temperatures that melt glaciers and warm oceans. Locally, deforestation in watersheds reduces water absorption, overpopulation increases water demand, industrial pollution contaminates rivers, and outdated irrigation infrastructure wastes enormous volumes of water.

Question 07

What steps can be taken to protect Pakistan's water bodies?

Solutions range from international climate action to local community practices. Pakistan needs strong early warning systems for glacial lake floods, restoration of mangrove forests, investment in water efficient farming (drip irrigation), and construction of water storage infrastructure to handle feast-or-famine river flows. Planting trees in mountain watersheds, treating industrial wastewater before it enters rivers, and educating communities about conservation are all vital local steps. Internationally, Pakistan continues to advocate for climate reparations from high-emitting nations responsible for these changes.
